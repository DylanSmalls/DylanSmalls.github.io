About Me

From writing code to understanding the machine underneath it.

My path to Georgia Tech started at Clark Atlanta University, where I spent three years earning my foundation in Computer Science and finished with a 3.69 GPA. Programming taught me how to tell a computer what to do, but I kept wondering what was actually happening underneath my code. How does a line of Java turn into electrical signals? Discovery Project · ECE 1100 · In Progress

ESP32 Multi-Subject Flashcard Device

A low-cost, handheld study device I am designing around an ESP32 microcontroller. It will store flashcard decks for multiple subjects, starting with Japanese vocabulary, and quiz me without the distractions of a phone.

  • ESP32
  • Hardware prototyping
  • Design thinking
Status

Design complete · Build starting

Prototype photos and results will be posted here as the build progresses.

The Problem

This semester I started learning Japanese, and the class has a vocabulary exam every few weeks. Flashcards are one of the best ways to memorize vocabulary, but both common options had problems for me. Paper cards are easy to lose, hard to shuffle by topic, and turn into a messy stack after a few chapters. Flashcard apps work well, but studying on my phone means notifications and other apps are always one tap away. I wanted a dedicated study tool that is portable, focused, and flexible enough to hold decks for any class, not just Japanese. It also had to be affordable for a student and something I could build and test in my dorm room.

The Process

I am following the design thinking process to move from an idea to a working prototype. The first three stages are complete, and the build is next.

1. Empathize ✓

I started with my own study habits while preparing for weekly vocabulary exams. What I needed most was quick review sessions, the ability to focus on one lesson at a time, and fewer distractions.

2. Define ✓

I defined the goal as a handheld device that displays a card, lets the user flip it to see the answer, marks whether they knew it, and moves to the next card, with decks organized by subject and lesson.

3. Ideate ✓

I chose the ESP32 because it is inexpensive, well documented, and has built-in Wi-Fi, which leaves room to load new decks wirelessly in a future version. I then planned how the buttons, screen, and storage will fit together (see the system diagram below) and presented the idea in my project pitch.

4. Prototype (next)

I will build the first prototype on a breadboard so I can change the wiring easily, then write the firmware that shows a card, flips it, and moves through a deck. The goal is to keep the total cost low enough for any student to build one.

5. Test (upcoming)

I will use the device to study for my Japanese vocabulary exams, note what slows me down or feels awkward, and improve the design based on what I find.

The Outcome So Far

The project is in progress. The problem, requirements, and system design are complete, and I am moving into the build. My goal for the Discovery Project Showcase is a working device that can store several decks and quiz me through a full Japanese vocabulary lesson. I will update this page with photos, results, and lessons learned as the build comes together.

What Comes Next

After the first working version, I want to use the ESP32's Wi-Fi to load new decks from my laptop, add a simple spaced-repetition system so the device shows harder cards more often, and use my 3D printing experience to design a case so it can travel in my backpack.

Research Team Capstone · Clark Atlanta University · Spring 2025

DOGUI.ai: Voice-Enabled AI Design Assistant

Role: Backend Developer and Cloud Engineer, ViSiDEL (Visual, Simulation, and Design Lab) Research Team

  • IBM watsonx
  • Speech-to-text
  • Backend development
  • Cloud

The Problem

Engineers working through the Engineering Design Process (ideation, simulation, and implementation) often have to jump between separate tools and resources. Our team set out to build DOGUI.ai, a GenAI website built on IBM watsonx that guides engineers through each stage of the design process with AI-assisted tools.

My Contribution

I engineered the speech-to-text pipeline and helped build the text-to-speech pipeline for the DOGUI.ai assistant using IBM watsonx APIs. Together, these let users speak naturally into a microphone and hear the AI's responses in real time, like a live conversation. I then integrated voice interaction into the website's core assistant feature.

The Outcome

The voice-enabled assistant was successfully demoed to IBM stakeholders and Clark Atlanta University leadership as part of our final project presentation. Working on a team taught me how to connect my piece of a system to everyone else's, and how much testing and troubleshooting it takes to make a live demo work.

More projects coming soon as I continue at Georgia Tech.
